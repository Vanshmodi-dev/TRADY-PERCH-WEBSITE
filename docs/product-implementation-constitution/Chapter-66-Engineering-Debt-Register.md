# CHAPTER 66 — ENGINEERING DEBT REGISTER

**Trady Perch Product Implementation Constitution · Part XIV: Governance & Continuous Improvement**

**Inherited From:** Design System Bible Chapter 69 (Design Debt Register & Management — Dd-1 "Every Documented Limitation Becomes a Register Entry," Dd-2 "Debt Is Prioritized by Blast Radius," Dd-3 "Resolution Is Logged With the Same Rigor as Creation"). Chapter 64 (Quality Governance Model) is this chapter's direct premise.

---

## 1. INTRODUCTION

This register has been cited throughout this Constitution as the destination for every logged exception, every "documented limitation," every gap between what a chapter requires and what the codebase currently does. This chapter is where that register is actually specified — its format, its prioritization, and its resolution discipline — so that the many individual citations scattered across sixty-five prior chapters all point to one real, consistently structured artifact rather than an assumed, informal notion of "tracking it somewhere."

---

## 2. WHAT BECOMES AN ENTRY

Per Dd-1, extended from design debt to engineering debt directly: every deviation from this Constitution's standard — a chapter's own documented limitation, a temporary exception logged per Chapter 62, a self-review or reconciliation gap per Chapter 32/53, a missed SLA per Chapter 45 §5 — becomes a register entry the moment it's identified, not retroactively once someone remembers to write it down. An identified deviation with no register entry is, per this chapter's own success criterion, itself treated as a governance failure when the deviation concerns a P0-priority chapter — the same severity this Constitution's own architecture document already assigns P0 status.

---

## 3. ENTRY FORMAT

Every entry states: the specific chapter and requirement being deviated from, the reason the deviation exists, its owner, and — per Dd-2 — its priority, determined by blast radius rather than by how recently or urgently it was raised. A P0-chapter deviation affecting a live, in-production surface per Chapter 2 is higher priority than a P3-chapter deviation in a not-yet-built future surface, regardless of which was logged first.

### Current Register (as of Milestone 7)

**[Chapter 19, §2–§6 — Assistive Technology Testing Protocol, entirely unperformed.]** Milestone 7 built and verified extensive Chapter 18 Layer 1 (static jsx-a11y lint) and Layer 2 (automated `vitest-axe` component tests + real-Chromium `axe-core`/Playwright route crawl, zero violations across all 29 routes) coverage. It did not, and structurally cannot, perform Chapter 19's Layer 3: real screen-reader walkthroughs and keyboard-only task completion judged by a human, with a dated pass/fail record per Ch.19 §4. This is a capability gap, not a negligence gap — Ch.19 §7 itself rejects a browser's accessibility-tree inspector as a stand-in for real assistive technology, and the same reasoning extends to automated scanning of any kind: no amount of additional automated tooling substitutes for a human actually listening to a screen reader and judging whether a flow is genuinely usable, which is precisely why Ch.19 exists as a distinct, non-automatable layer. *Reason:* the work in this milestone was performed by an AI agent with no access to real assistive-technology hardware/software or auditory judgment. *Owner:* unassigned — requires a human with real screen-reader access (NVDA, JAWS, or VoiceOver) to be named before this can close. *Priority:* high blast radius — per UX/Experience Blueprint Chapter 31's flow-priority model, the Contact form (the site's stated "terminal conversion point") and primary navigation are near-certain P0/P1 flows, and Ch.19 §6 states a release "does not proceed" without a current-cycle passing record for every P0/P1 flow. This does not block continued autonomous milestone work, but it does block a genuine production launch per Ch.19's own release-gate language. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* before public launch, a human tester runs Ch.19 §2's full protocol (screen-reader walkthrough + keyboard-only task completion + touch/gesture verification on real hardware) against at minimum the Contact flow and primary navigation, and logs the dated result per Ch.19 §4.

**[Chapter 36 §3 — shared/vendor JS bundle exceeds its 150KB gzip ceiling, entirely attributable to the framework, not application code.]** Milestone 8's real-browser CDP measurement (`apps/marketing-site/scripts/performance-audit.mjs`) found the shared bundle at ~165KB gzip across all 29 routes. Direct inspection of `.next/build-manifest.json`'s `rootMainFiles` (Next.js's own React/router/hydration runtime) plus its `polyfillFiles` entry, summed at their real gzip sizes, accounts for ~168KB — i.e. essentially the entire measured total; this project's own shared application code (Header, Footer, Drawer, Reveal) contributes negligibly beyond that baseline. A `browserslist` field (`"defaults", "not IE 11", "not dead"`) was added to `apps/marketing-site/package.json` and empirically tested (full rebuild, byte-for-byte chunk comparison) specifically to shrink the ~39.5KB `polyfillFiles` chunk — it produced zero measurable change, and no polyfill-related option was found in this Next.js version's documented `next.config` type definitions or webpack config source, either. *Reason:* Turbopack (Next.js 16's bundler) does not currently appear to support browserslist-aware differential polyfill bundling the way the older webpack-based Next.js build did — a toolchain-version limitation, not an application-code defect. The `browserslist` field was reverted after the empirical test showed no effect, rather than left in as a no-op. *Owner:* unassigned — requires either a Next.js/Turbopack version bump once this capability lands upstream, or a deliberate, reviewed decision to eject to webpack for this one build concern. *Priority:* structural — affects every route's shared bundle, but the gap versus budget (~15KB) is narrow relative to the ~150KB floor, and TTFB/CLS are excellent across the board per the same measurement run. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* re-test after any future Next.js major/minor upgrade by re-running `npm run test:performance --workspace=@trady-perch/marketing-site` and re-checking the polyfill chunk's size; close once it either shrinks under a modern-browserslist target or Turbopack documents an explicit opt-out.

**[Chapter 36 §2 — Largest Contentful Paint exceeds its 2000ms budget, under Lighthouse's simulated mobile-CPU throttling, on every route (not only the homepage as Milestone 8 originally, incorrectly, recorded).]** See [[0008-ceremonial-intro-lcp-budget-tension]] (including its Milestone 10 correction section) for the full record; this entry replaces and corrects that ADR's original "narrowly scoped... every other route unaffected" claim. Summary: the homepage's own LCP element is the ceremonial intro sequence's logo image (Master Vision §9.2), whose Load-Delay defect was fixed in Milestone 8 (image and preload now render unconditionally from first server paint, not gated behind a client-side `sessionStorage` check) — a real, verified win, still true. What Milestone 8 got wrong: it treated the homepage's remaining ~3s "Render Delay" as unique to the ceremonial sequence, when Milestone 10's full 29-route audit found every route — all of which have zero page-specific JS, per the same run's bundle table — carries an LCP of 3038–4239ms. The actual, shared root cause is the ~165KB shared/vendor JS bundle (already logged below, this entry's neighbor) taking that long to parse and hydrate under Lighthouse's default 4x CPU-throttled simulation, independent of any route's own content or the intro sequence specifically. Confirmed via a real, unthrottled `PerformanceObserver` measurement against a live production server (`scripts/lcp-check.mjs`, a one-off diagnostic): `/` 276ms, `/legal` 208ms, `/pricing` 220ms, `/solutions/ai-agents` 228ms — genuinely excellent for a real visitor on real hardware; this is a lab-methodology characteristic, not a user-facing regression. *Reason:* Ch.48 §3 mandates Lighthouse-equivalent lab measurement specifically (a real browser under representative, not idealized, conditions) as this budget's enforcement mechanism, and the shared bundle's size — already an open, disclosed toolchain limitation — is what that representative condition is now catching sitewide, not merely on the one route that happened to get scrutinized closely in Milestone 8. The homepage's own ceremonial pacing is still real and still additive on top of this shared cost (it measures worst, at 4239ms vs. ~3000–3600ms elsewhere) — ADR-0008's underlying Decision (keep the pacing, don't shorten a deliberate brand moment to game a lab metric) stands unchanged; only the claimed scope was wrong. *Owner:* unassigned — the shared-bundle entry below is this issue's actual point of resolution; whoever owns Master Vision §9.2 revisions owns the homepage's additional, deliberate portion specifically. *Priority:* structural, sitewide — every route is affected, not one; but real-world/unthrottled experience is confirmed excellent, so user-facing urgency is lower than the lab number alone would suggest. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* shrinking the shared bundle (see the neighboring entry's resolution path — a Turbopack/Next.js version bump once browserslist-aware polyfill trimming lands) is the actual fix for the sitewide portion; the homepage's own additional ceremonial-pacing cost has no resolution path pursued unless Master Vision §9.2 itself changes. `performance-audit.mjs` will continue reporting every route as a violation by design under this methodology — do not "fix" by loosening the script's threshold, switching to unthrottled measurement, or excluding routes from measurement.

**[Design System Bible Chapter 55, Pf-2 — no hardware-capability animation-degradation path exists, only the `prefers-reduced-motion` trigger.]** See [[0009-pf2-reduced-motion-as-sole-degradation-signal]] for the full record. Pf-2 names two distinct triggers for simplifying an animation on request — a user's `prefers-reduced-motion` preference (implemented sitewide, per Ch.15 Mt-4) and a device's own low hardware capability (not implemented anywhere). *Reason:* the only realistic browser signals for hardware capability (`navigator.hardwareConcurrency`, `navigator.deviceMemory`) are increasingly fingerprint-resistant/rounded/absent by design in modern browsers, making a threshold-based check unreliable in exactly the browsers most likely to need it; a real runtime dropped-frame heuristic is a legitimate alternative but a nontrivial feature of its own, requiring a design pass Master Vision §9.2 hasn't specified. Building an unreliable version under milestone time pressure was judged worse than disclosing the gap. *Owner:* unassigned. *Priority:* narrowly scoped — the only Ceremonial-tier animation in the system is the homepage intro sequence; every other animated element sitewide runs at Standard tier or below. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* revisit once a reliable, non-fingerprinting capability signal exists, or once real production data shows this is a genuine problem for real visitors — not before, per Chapter 1's IP3.

**[Chapter 41 §5 — the periodic third-party AI spot-check has not been performed.]** Milestone 9 built Section 2's actual machine-readable capability description (`apps/marketing-site/public/llms.txt`, plus Chapter 40 §3 structured data extended to `Service`/`Article` blocks per this chapter's own cross-reference) and wrote it to Section 3's structural-legibility standard — plain factual statements about services, industries, and engagement model, sourced directly from `solutions-data.ts`/`industries-data.ts`/`pricing-data.ts` rather than independently authored. It did not, and structurally cannot, perform Section 5's actual check: querying real third-party AI systems and judging whether their summaries of Trady Perch stay consistent with Brand Identity Manual Chapter 14's messaging pillars. This is the same category of gap as the Chapter 19 entry above — Section 5 itself calls this "not a fully automatable process," and an AI agent producing the content being checked is not an independent check of it. *Reason:* the work in this milestone was performed by an AI agent with no ability to independently query and evaluate other production AI systems' outputs about Trady Perch. *Owner:* unassigned — requires a human to run Section 5's spot-check cadence against real systems (e.g. asking a general-purpose AI assistant "what does Trady Perch do?" and comparing the answer against Ch.14's pillars) before this can close. *Priority:* moderate blast radius — `llms.txt` and the structured-data blocks are net-new, real, correct content either way; the open item is verifying a third party actually reads and represents them accurately, which has no bearing on whether the rest of the site functions correctly. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* before or shortly after public launch, a human runs Section 5's spot-check against at least one mainstream AI assistant, logs the result and any divergence found, and repeats on Chapter 106's established cadence.

**[Chapters 49, 55–59 — no CI/CD pipeline exists; every automated gate these chapters describe as "wired into CI" currently runs manually, by whoever remembers to run it, not on every pull request.]** Milestone 10's final-QA pass confirmed: `npm run typecheck`, `lint`, `test`, `test:a11y`, `test:keyboard`, `test:performance`, and `test:schema` all exist and all pass, but none of them run automatically anywhere — there is no `.github/workflows/` directory, no branch-protection configuration, and no repository host beyond the local working tree (this repo has one real commit; everything since is uncommitted, per this project's own standing instruction not to push without explicit authorization). Chapter 49 §5 requires the gate sequence be enforced "structurally, not by convention," via branch protection with no override permission; Chapter 43 §12 requires a SAST tool wired into that same pipeline; Chapter 45 §4 requires a scheduled, automatic dependency-vulnerability scan (an `npm audit --omit=dev` was run manually this milestone and found zero vulnerabilities, but that is exactly the "manual, periodic audit someone has to remember to run" this chapter explicitly says its requirement is not). *Reason:* setting up a CI/CD platform account, a SAST tool subscription, and branch-protection rules on a real hosted repository are operations-layer, account-owning decisions (which CI provider, which SAST vendor, who holds admin rights) outside what an AI agent building website content and application code can decide or provision unilaterally — and this milestone plan's own ten-item scope (Setup through Final QA) was scoped to the product itself, never named standing up hosted CI/CD infrastructure as a deliverable. This is the single root cause behind what would otherwise be five or six separate "gate doesn't actually block anything" entries — logged once, here, rather than fragmented. *Owner:* unassigned — requires a human with hosting/CI-provider account access to choose a provider (e.g. GitHub Actions, given the repo's git history), wire up the scripts this milestone already built as CI jobs, configure branch protection per Ch.49 §5, and select/configure a SAST tool per Ch.43 §12. *Priority:* high blast radius — this is the enforcement mechanism nearly every other quality chapter (18, 36, 40, 43, 45, 47–48) assumes exists; every individual check it would run is itself real, built, and passing today, but nothing currently prevents a future change from silently breaking one without being caught before merge. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* once a repository host and CI provider are chosen (a Chapter 62 ADR-worthy decision), translate this milestone's existing `npm run` scripts directly into CI job steps in the order Chapter 49 §2 specifies, add branch-protection rules requiring each to pass, and add a scheduled dependency-scan job per Ch.45 §4 — no new checks need inventing, only wiring the ones already built into an actual automated pipeline.

**[Product Implementation Constitution Ch.28 §5 — the required automated lint rule for fabricated loading-state delays does not exist.]** Ch.28 §5 names a specific enforcement mechanism: "A lint rule flags any `setTimeout`, `setInterval`, or equivalent artificial-delay construct found inside a loading-state code path, distinguishing it from Section 3's narrow, explicitly annotated flicker-guard exception." No such rule exists in `apps/marketing-site/eslint.config.mjs` or `packages/config/eslint.base.mjs`. Milestone 8's own verification that the codebase currently has zero fabricated delays was a manual, one-time audit (direct grep + read of every `setTimeout` call site), not the automated, permanent mechanism the chapter names — a currently-clean codebase with no regression protection. *Reason:* a custom ESLint rule capable of distinguishing "a loading-state code path" from any other `setTimeout` use requires real semantic understanding (which callers are rendering a loading/skeleton state) that a syntactic lint rule can't cheaply infer; this is a nontrivial rule-authoring task, not a quick fix, and wasn't scoped into this milestone's build. Pre-existing gap, not introduced by Milestone 8. *Owner:* unassigned. *Priority:* structural — the chapter treats this as this rule's primary enforcement mechanism, currently absent entirely. *Status: open.* *Resolution path:* author a project-specific ESLint rule (or a scoped `no-restricted-syntax` pattern targeting known loading-state file/component conventions) once those conventions are established firmly enough to detect reliably; until then, re-run the same manual audit this milestone performed at each subsequent milestone's review pass.

## 4. PRIORITIZATION BY BLAST RADIUS

Per Dd-2, applied here exactly as Design System Bible Chapter 69 already applies it to design debt: a deviation's priority is a function of how many other parts of the system depend on the standard being deviated from, and how severe the consequence of the deviation actually is — not a function of urgency alone. A deviation in a foundational chapter (Chapter 1, Chapter 2, Chapter 7) that other chapters build directly on top of carries inherently higher blast radius than an equivalent deviation in a narrower, less-depended-upon chapter, mirroring Chapter 64 §2's Gov-4 principle applied to debt specifically rather than to decision authority.

---

## 5. RESOLUTION

Per Dd-3, resolving a debt entry is logged with the same rigor as creating one — what was actually done, when, and verified against the entry's original stated requirement, never simply deleted once someone believes it's been addressed. A resolved entry remains in the register's history rather than disappearing, so a pattern of similar entries recurring over time (per Chapter 65 §4's amendment-trigger logic) remains visible across resolved and open entries alike, not only the currently-open ones.

---

## 6. ENFORCEMENT & MEASUREMENT

This chapter's own success criterion — every P0-chapter deviation has a corresponding entry — is checked directly per Chapter 65's quarterly cadence, cross-referencing every P0 chapter's own stated requirements against the register for gaps with no corresponding entry. An unregistered P0 deviation discovered this way is treated with the severity this chapter's own opening section already states — a governance failure, escalated per Chapter 64 rather than quietly added to the register as though its earlier absence were unremarkable.

## 7. BEHAVIORAL RULES

**When any deviation from this Constitution is identified, by anyone or any process.** A register entry is created immediately per Section 3 — never deferred until a convenient moment, which per Chapter 5 §2 (F2) is exactly how an undocumented exception starts.

**When prioritizing the register for attention.** Section 4's blast-radius model is used, not recency or the loudest current complaint.

**When a debt entry is resolved.** Section 5's resolution-logging discipline is followed in full, with verification against the entry's original requirement, before it's marked closed.

---

## 8. DO / DON'T

**Do** create a register entry the moment a deviation is identified, regardless of how minor it seems.

**Do** prioritize the register by blast radius per Section 4, not by whichever entry is most recent or most loudly raised.

**Don't** delete a resolved entry — log its resolution per Dd-3 and keep it in the register's history.

**Don't** let a P0-chapter deviation go unregistered — this is treated as a governance failure, not an oversight to fix quietly.
