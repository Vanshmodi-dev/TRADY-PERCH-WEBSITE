**Tagline (verbatim, appears repeatedly, always styled as three capitalized commands with periods):**

**Positioning statement — prospect's-mind version (verbatim):**

**What Trady Perch actually does/sells (literal, from Master Vision Ch. 13.5 and 11.2):**

- Sells against named operational pain points: "manual data entry, missed follow-ups, slow quote turnaround, disconnected tools."

- Named target industries/verticals (used for the "Industries" homepage section and for future SEO landing content): **real estate, medical, legal, manufacturing, education, finance, e-commerce.**
- Process, as referenced (not fully detailed): a small number of clear stages, example given as "Discover → Design → Build → Deploy → Support."

## Target Audience

No formal named personas (e.g., "Persona 1: Maria the Ops Manager") appear in either document. The audience is instead defined through buyer psychology and category framing:

- **Core buyer:** "an established business... losing time and money to manual processes" — a **business owner or CEO** evaluating an AI automation partner, described repeatedly as "a skeptical, busy CEO."
- **Purchase profile:** explicitly a **high-consideration, high-price decision**, "often requiring the buyer to convince a colleague or a partner before committing, with a genuine and reasonable fear of a bad outcome if they choose the wrong partner" (Section 5.1) — contrasted directly against low-consideration impulse-purchase psychology, which the document says must never be used on this audience (no urgency, no countdowns, no pop-ups).
- **What they care about, in explicit priority order (the "Trust Equation," Section 2.3):**
  1. Evidence of past outcomes ("has this been done before, for someone like me, with a measurable result?")
  2. Evidence of process rigor ("a repeatable, professional method")
  3. Evidence of technical depth ("do they actually understand the systems they're proposing to touch?")
  4. Aesthetic polish (explicitly ranked last — "a hygiene factor, not a decision factor")
- **The buyer's literal internal monologue** the whole homepage is built to answer, in order (Section 11.2): "What is this, in one sentence?" → "Do they actually have real technical capability, or is this marketing?" → "Do they understand my specific pain, or are they generic?" → "Okay, so what do they actually build?" → "Have they worked with businesses like mine?" → "What happens if I say yes?" → "Show me. Proof, not promises." → "Can I experience this before committing to a call?" → "What do other real clients say?" → "Roughly what does this cost, and is it going to feel fair?" → "What are the objections I still haven't voiced?" → "How do I actually start?"
- **Target verticals:** real estate, medical, legal, manufacturing, education, finance, e-commerce.
- **Competitive/aspirational frame of reference** (style and posture peers, not necessarily direct competitors): Apple, Stripe, Linear, Vercel, Framer, Tesla, Nothing, Arc — named specifically because "every one of them made the deliberate choice to look less busy than their competitors while charging more."
- **What Trady Perch is explicitly positioned against:** (1) "the low-trust freelancer portfolio site (busy, self-congratulatory, template-driven)" and (2) "the over-engineered enterprise SaaS site (feature-dense, jargon-heavy, cold)." Also explicitly not competing on "AI capability" as an axis (called "noisy, jargon-flooded, and largely undifferentiated") but on **"operator credibility"** — "does this look like a company a CFO would sign a contract with?"

## Brand Voice & Tone

**Personality brief terms (as given, before distillation):** confident, elegant, premium, innovative, trustworthy, modern, minimal, luxury, professional, intelligent, calm, purposeful.

**These collapse into three governing traits (Section 2.2), which the document says every design and copy decision must be checked against:**
1. **Composed** — "never rushes, never raises its voice, and never over-explains."
2. **Precise** — "Every word, every pixel of spacing, every millisecond of animation timing is deliberate."
3. **Quietly powerful** — "Gold is used exactly like a watch bezel is used — as a signal of value that is seen more than it is noticed."

**What the brand is never allowed to be (verbatim list, Section 2.2):** "playful, cute, meme-driven, jargon-stacked, hype-driven ('revolutionary,' 'game-changing,' '10x'), cluttered, neon, gradient-heavy, or eager." Eagerness is called out specifically: "the single most damaging tone available to this brand."

**Copywriting rules (Chapter 16.1):**
- Write "the way a competent senior engineer explains a solution to a smart but non-technical business owner: clear, direct, respectful of their intelligence, never condescending, never over-explaining."
- Test for every sentence: "Would a skeptical, busy CEO find this sentence worth their time, or would they skim past it as filler?" Delete if not.
- **Avoid buzzword stacking** — explicit bad-example given: *"leverage cutting-edge AI-powered synergistic automation."* Say the specific thing instead.
- **Avoid empty superlatives**: "world-class," "best-in-class," "revolutionary."
- First person confident, not boastful — example given: **"we build systems that remove the busywork"** (good) vs. **"we are the leading experts in AI transformation"** (bad).

**Headline doctrine (16.2) — every headline must pass three tests:**
1. Specificity test — could a generic competitor use this exact headline unchanged? If yes, rewrite.
2. Benefit test — outcome for the reader, not a company feature. Example given: **"Get quotes to customers in minutes, not days"** beats **"AI-powered quoting engine."**
3. Economy test — can any word be cut without losing meaning?

**Section-by-section copy direction (16.3):**
- Hero: one sentence stating the transformation delivered, not a service list; complements (doesn't repeat) the tagline.
- Problems We Solve: second person ("you"), concrete daily friction.
- Solutions: outcome-first — lead with what changes for the business, then what was built.
- How We Work: short, numbered, confident.
- Pricing Philosophy: transparent about *how* pricing works even without exact numbers; "vagueness here reads as evasiveness."
- FAQ: real objections, answered directly, "never softened into non-answers."
- CTA copy: action-oriented, low-pressure — examples given: **"Book a Strategy Call," "See It In Action"** — vs. forbidden **"Act now," "Limited spots."**

**Words/phrases explicitly to avoid (compiled from across the document):** "revolutionary," "game-changing," "10x," "world-class," "best-in-class," "leverage cutting-edge AI-powered synergistic automation," "Act now," "Limited spots," "only 2 spots left," "offer ends tonight," and any variant of "trustworthy" used as a self-applied adjective (Chapter 28 explicitly forbids the word "trustworthy" appearing in the copy at all — trust must be demonstrated, never asserted).

**Nav/CTA label options given but explicitly unfinalized:** "Book a Call" / "Start a Project" — "exact label to be finalized in copy work" (Section 12.2).

**AI/chat assistant voice (Chapter 19 — "AI Personality Constitution"), for any conversational surface (demo, future chat assistant):**
- Same three traits as the brand: composed, precise, quietly powerful.
- Short, direct sentences; plain business vocabulary unless the visitor signals technical fluency; confident declarative statements, not hedged ("I think," "it's possible that").
- **Never use exclamation marks, never use emoji.** No forced warmth ("Great question!," "Happy to help!").
- Responses as short as fully answering the question allows — never padded.
- Greeting: brief, calm, functional. Closing: low-pressure next step, e.g. **"If it would help, I can have someone from the team follow up"** vs. forbidden **"Don't miss out — book your call now!"**
- Humor: almost entirely absent; if present, dry/restrained wit only — never jokes about being an AI, never internet-culture references.
- On not knowing something: say so plainly — **"I don't have that information, but I can find out"** is correct; apologetic hedging is not.
- Must never sound like: "a hyped-up sales bot, a customer-support script reading from a decision tree, a caricature of 'friendly AI' with exclamation marks and emoji, or a nervous, over-hedging assistant apologizing for its own existence" (19.10).

## Brand Identity Fundamentals

*Note: concrete specs below come from the Master Vision Document (Chapter 6 and Chapter 8), because the Brand Identity Manual Architecture document does not itself contain literal color values, logo construction grids, or chosen typefaces — see the note at the top of this file. Where the Architecture document adds a governing concept not present in the Master Vision, it is marked "[Brand Identity Manual]" below.*

### Logo usage rules
- The logo is a **"TP" monogram** — "constructed the way a luxury wordmark is constructed: as a small number of clean geometric strokes with confident negative space," capable of being "drawn" stroke-by-stroke in the intro animation.
- Must work **unmodified in spirit at three sizes**: full-screen intro hero, standard nav-bar scale, and favicon scale. Explicit rule: "if a version of the mark's detail disappears at favicon size, the mark is too complex and must be simplified, not have a 'simplified favicon variant' bolted on."
- Gold is reserved for, among other things, "the logo mark."
- **[Brand Identity Manual] "Recognition Thesis" (Ch. 2):** the minimum cue-set that must survive even when every other identity element is stripped away is named as: wordmark silhouette, the black-and-gold relationship, and the composed voice register — described as needing to survive "a black-and-white fax, a one-color booth banner."
- **[Brand Identity Manual]** Plans (not yet written) for a full geometric construction standard (proportions, baseline grid, minimum size across screen/offset print/embroidery/laser-etching) live in a future Chapter 21, and clear-space/exclusion-zone and logo-misuse-catalog chapters (22–23) — none of this construction detail exists yet in authored form.

### Core color identity
From Master Vision Section 6.1 (explicit color table):

| Role | Direction | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Primary Background | Deep Matte Black, near-black, "e.g. #0A0A0B – #0C0C0E range, never pure #000000" | Reads as "designed," not "default dark mode." |
| Secondary Background | Charcoal Black, one perceptible step lighter | Separates sections without hard borders. |
| Card Surface | Dark Graphite, one further step lighter, with hairline border | Cards read as physical panels. |
| Primary Accent | **Luxury Metallic Gold** | The single most brand-defining choice. Used only for: logo mark, key CTAs, active/selected states, and result numerals (e.g., "40% faster"). |
| Secondary Accent | Soft Champagne Gold | Desaturated/lighter; subheadlines, hover glows, thin decorative lines. |
| Primary Text | Off-white, "e.g. #F5F4F1" | Warm-tinted, not pure white. |
| Secondary Text | Cool Gray | Supporting copy, captions, metadata. |
| Success | Elegant Emerald | Confirmation states only. |
| Error | Minimal Crimson | Validation/error states only, desaturated. |

**Explicit hard rules:**
- **Gold must never exceed roughly 10% of any given screen's surface area** — "the moment gold covers a large surface area, it stops feeling premium and starts feeling gaudy."
- Gradients permitted only as extremely subtle, near-imperceptible transitions — never an obvious two-color blend background fill.
- **No neon, no saturated blue/purple "tech" accent** — explicitly called "the uniform of every generic SaaS competitor."
- Gold is spent as a deliberate "budget" across the scroll (Section 7.4): minimal in the hero, nearly absent through informational sections, reintroduced at proof/decision moments — never evenly distributed.

**[Brand Identity Manual]** flags that a future Chapter 26 ("Color Identity Beyond Digital") will need to define Pantone spot-color and CMYK equivalents for print — these conversions do not exist yet in any source document reviewed. It also states the Manual's own color system must always derive from a separate "Design System Bible" token source (Ch. 2–3), which was outside this task's scope.

### Typography identity
From Master Vision Section 6.2:
- **Primary typeface direction:** "a refined geometric or grotesque sans-serif with excellent optical weight range (light through semibold), similar in spirit to the families used by Linear, Vercel, and Stripe (e.g., **Inter, General Sans, Neue Montreal**, or a comparable premium grotesque)." No single typeface is definitively chosen — this is a directional brief, not a final selection.
- Display/hero weight: extra-large, **light-to-regular (never bold)**.
- Hierarchy discipline: **no more than four distinct type sizes visible in any single viewport.**
- Letter-spacing: slightly widened tracking on all-caps labels (section eyebrows, nav items, the tagline itself).
- Line length: body copy constrained to ~60–75 characters per line even on wide desktop viewports.
- Numerals: tabular, slightly oversized, **in gold**, wherever a metric/result appears.

**[Brand Identity Manual]** flags (future Ch. 27, not yet written) that webfont licensing does not automatically cover print/signage/engraving use, and that this must be resolved separately before physical production — a legal-risk note, not a spec.

### Imagery / photography / illustration style principles
From Master Vision Section 6.4 and Chapter 8 ("Art Direction Bible"):
- **Iconography:** thin-stroke, geometric, monoline only — no filled/glyph icons, no illustrated mascots, no emoji. White or cool gray by default; gold reserved for "active"/highlighted state only.
- **Imagery direction:** "abstract, dark, moody photography or renders (subtle metallic textures, soft light studies, dark architectural or workspace photography)" over literal stock photography.
- **Explicitly forbidden:** stock photography of "business people shaking hands," "team high-fiving at a laptop," "generic diverse office teams," "lightbulbs-as-ideas," and — called out specifically as a category cliché — **robot/humanoid AI imagery** ("makes the brand look like it is selling science fiction rather than operational software").
- **Lighting philosophy (8.1):** one dominant, soft-but-directional light source per image (Rembrandt-style); deep shadow retained, not lifted in post; highlights fall only where the composition wants attention.
- **Photography philosophy (8.2):** subjects occupy a minority of the frame with generous dark negative space; slightly elevated/eye-level camera, never a dramatic low "hero" angle; desaturated, cool-to-neutral grading; gold only as a genuine light source/reflection, never a color-grade filter; no visible third-party logos, no "corporate headshot" eye contact energy.
- **3D/render philosophy (8.3):** privilege material realism (brushed/polished metal, dark glass, film grain) over geometric spectacle — "when in doubt, render fewer objects, more convincingly."
- **Camera/lens language (8.4):** longer-lens optical character — flatter perspective, minimal distortion, shallow elegant blur — never wide-angle distortion.
- **Materials (8.5):** narrow, consistent vocabulary — brushed/polished metal, dark smoked/frosted glass, matte textured black; a very light amount of film grain/noise throughout (avoids "sterile... AI-generated placeholder" look).
- **Screenshot/device treatment (8.7):** product/dashboard screenshots must sit inside a minimal, dark device or browser frame — "never as raw, unframed rectangles."
- **[Brand Identity Manual]** future Chapter 28 ("Photographic & Imagery Identity Marker") frames imagery as a recognition cue that should let a stranger identify a Trady Perch image "even without a visible logo" — a governing goal, not yet a resolved spec.

## Differentiators & Proof Points

- **Central strategic thesis (Ch. 1, 3.1):** "restraint is the strongest form of persuasion available to a technical company." Core operating rule: **"If in doubt, remove it."**
- **Positioning differentiator:** "operates like a private bank, not like a marketplace freelancer" — explicitly *not* claiming cheapest, fastest, or most cutting-edge, because those are "commodity claims that any competitor can copy in a sentence."
- **Competitive axis chosen deliberately:** operator credibility, not AI capability — "does this look like a company a CFO would sign a contract with?"
- **Trust Equation** (Section 2.3) as the explicit hierarchy of proof, in order of weight: (1) past outcomes, (2) process rigor, (3) technical depth, (4) aesthetic polish.
- **"No claim without evidence nearby"** is listed as one of the twelve Non-Negotiable Design Principles (Chapter 27, item 7) — superlatives and unproven claims are explicitly disallowed.
- **Case study / portfolio narrative spine (Chapter 15)** — every project must be structured through these seven fixed elements: (1) Business Problem (stated in client language, e.g. "Sales reps were spending 12 hours a week manually qualifying inbound leads," not "Needed a lead-scoring pipeline"), (2) Solution, (3) Technologies Used (never given more visual weight than the outcome), (4) Implementation Process, (5) Business Impact, (6) Measurable Results (quantified wherever possible — "hours saved per week, response-time reduction, conversion lift, cost reduction" — called "the single highest-trust content type on the entire website"), (7) **Lessons Learned** — an explicitly unusual, deliberate inclusion: "a company confident enough to admit a real lesson from a real engagement reads as far more credible than one presenting every project as a flawless success story."
- **No actual case studies, client names, testimonials, or real metrics are provided in either source document.** All examples given (e.g., "40% faster," "Sales reps were spending 12 hours a week...") are illustrative placeholders, not real proof points. This is a genuine content gap a builder must source separately.
- **Style/positioning reference brands** (not competitors, but named aspirational parity): Apple, Stripe, Linear, Vercel, Framer, Tesla, Nothing, Arc.
- **The "Impossible Standard" (Chapter 28)** — the summary test for every decision: "does this make Trady Perch look like it needs to try harder to be believed, or does it make Trady Perch look like it already doesn't need to?" The stated goal is for the site to feel **"inevitable. Not impressive."**

## Required Pages / Business Priorities

**Launch pages (Section 14.1 — explicit):**
- Homepage, structured per the fixed 15-item Chapter 13 blueprint, in this exact order: (1) Navigation, (2) Hero, (3) Technology Stack, (4) Problems We Solve, (5) Solutions, (6) Industries, (7) How We Work, (8) Portfolio, (9) Case Studies, (10) Interactive AI Demo, (11) Testimonials, (12) Pricing Philosophy, (13) FAQ, (14) Contact, (15) Footer. Each is tied to a specific unspoken visitor question (Section 11.2) and a specific emotional beat (Section 4.3) — the document states no section may exist unless it maps to one of these questions, and any proposed new section must justify itself against this list.
- **Case Studies** (index + individual pages) — explicitly must exist at launch, not deferred: "Even two or three strong, real case studies outperform zero."
- **Privacy Policy & Terms of Service** — must exist at launch; their absence is called "a credibility red flag."
- **Contact / Book a Call** — if it needs to be a dedicated page/flow (e.g. scheduling integration) rather than an in-page section, it must exist at launch as "the terminal conversion point of the entire site."

**Deferred pages (Section 14.2 — explicit, with rationale):**
- **About** — Phase 2, not launch. Rationale: "a thin About page ('We are a passionate team...') actively damages credibility more than having no About page at all." Load-bearing "who we are" content should live inside Technology/How We Work sections instead, at launch.
- **Blog / Resources / Knowledge Base** — explicitly deferred until a sustainable publishing cadence exists; "an agency site with three blog posts from a year ago is worse for trust than no blog at all."
- **Careers** — deferred until a real, current hiring need exists.

**Other explicit business/build rules:**
- **Single primary CTA only**, no competing CTAs anywhere on a given screen (Sections 5.2, 11.1).
- **Gold is the only legitimate CTA color** (Section 5.3) — reserved specifically so it retains meaning.
- **No price shown anywhere before the dedicated Pricing Philosophy section** — not in hero, nav, or a floating element, "no matter how many well-meaning stakeholders ask for pricing transparency 'up front'" (Section 5.4).
- **No urgency/scarcity tactics anywhere** — countdowns, "only 2 spots left," etc. — called out as a non-negotiable prohibition (Section 5.5, Chapter 27 item 6), not a style preference.
- **Minimal primary navigation** — example items given: Solutions, Industries, Work, Pricing, Contact — explicitly never a mega-menu.
- **Mobile-first requirement** (Chapter 21) — not a "responsive afterthought retrofit."
- **Accessibility:** WCAG AA minimum contrast (AAA preferred for body copy), full keyboard operability, visible focus states, reduced-motion fallback required for all decorative motion including the intro sequence.
- **Performance:** strong Core Web Vitals treated as a "launch-blocking quality bar, not a post-launch optimization pass."
- **Intro animation contract:** shown once per session maximum, always skippable/interruptible, must degrade to an instant static presentation under reduced-motion settings (never skipped entirely — content must still be delivered).
- **The "Twelve Non-Negotiable Design Principles" (Chapter 27)** function as a hard build checklist: gold is accent-only (never a theme); every section must answer a named question and install a named feeling; every animation must be diegetic; no stock photos of generic business people/lightbulbs/robots; no page launched in a thin/placeholder state; no urgency/pressure tactics; no claim without nearby evidence; no accessibility/performance shortcuts for visual effect; no more than one dominant idea/motion per screen; intro shown once, always interruptible; no AI surface may depart from the AI Personality Constitution; no brand surface (dashboard, invoice, proposal, social) may fail the "One-Brand Test."
