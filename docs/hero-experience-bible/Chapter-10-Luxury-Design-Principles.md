# CHAPTER 10 — LUXURY DESIGN PRINCIPLES

**Trady Perch Hero Experience Bible · Part III: Premium & Identity**
*First-canonical-draft. Implementation-independent.*

**Inherited From:** Master Vision §2.2 (the watch-bezel analogy), §6.1 (≤10% gold), §7.4, §8.5, and the positioning: "operates like a private bank, not like a marketplace freelancer."
**Governs:** The signalling economics of the hero — what its restraint communicates about standing, and how scarcity is spent.
**Does Not Govern:** Craft quality under stress (Ch. 9) or the material vocabulary itself (Ch. 24).

---

## 1. THE POSITION

Chapter 9 answered *is this well made?* This chapter answers *what does this surface claim about the company's standing, and is it credible?* A hero can be impeccably made and still signal the wrong position — beautifully crafted, covered in badges and offers, premium in execution and mass-market in posture.

A private bank's materials are not better-designed than a retail bank's; they are *differently positioned*. They withhold. They do not list features, display prices, or offer a promotion. They assume you already know why you are there, and that assumption is the product.

**Costly signalling.** Signals carry information only when costly to fake. Anyone can claim quality; only a company with real standing can afford to *forgo* the things that generate short-term response. A restrained hero gives up urgency (which converts), a second CTA (which captures intent), a logo strip (which borrows credibility), a bold claim (which is remembered), an animated statistic (which impresses). The fast system reads the aggregate correctly: *this company can afford not to do those things.*

**But restraint is only a luxury signal when the alternative was available.** A hero that is quiet because there are no logos, metrics, or case studies is not signalling standing — it is displaying an absence, and a sophisticated buyer can tell. This is the honest tension: Trady Perch has no published proof content yet. Until proof exists further down the page, the hero's restraint is doing more work than it should, and the correct response is to source the proof rather than make the hero louder.

**The bezel principle.** §2.2's analogy is the most useful sentence in the source material: gold is used "exactly like a watch bezel is used — as a signal of value that is seen more than it is noticed." A bezel is a small quantity of expensive material at the *point of function* — the edge framing the dial you actually read. Not the strap, not the case back. Three obligations follow:

1. **Quantity is capped** — never above ~10% of a screen, because "the moment gold covers a large surface area, it stops feeling premium and starts feeling gaudy."
2. **Placement is functional** — gold marks the action, the result, the mark; never a border, divider, or flourish.
3. **The hero spends least** — §7.4: "minimal in the hero, nearly absent through informational sections, reintroduced at proof/decision moments."

**Withholding as positioning.** Master Vision has already made three such refusals: no price before the Pricing section (§5.4, "no matter how many well-meaning stakeholders ask"); no urgency or scarcity, permanently (§5.5); no About page at launch (§14.2, because a thin one "actively damages credibility more than having no About page at all"). The hero's own refusals — no badge, logo strip, metric, or second CTA — are the same act at smaller scale.

## 2. CORE PRINCIPLES

**Lx-1 — Scarcity is the source of signal value.** Every emphatic device derives meaning from how rarely it is used; each additional use devalues every prior one. §6.1 reserves gold for the logo mark, key CTAs, active states, and result numerals, and §5.3 makes it "the only legitimate CTA colour." Those reservations are the entire basis of its value. *Scarcity means rationed against a stated budget, with each spend deliberate — not a minimum-use rule.*

**Lx-2 — Value is seen more than it is noticed.** A signal that becomes the subject of attention has failed twice: it spent attention the claim needed, and made the company's effort visible. *Exception: the intro's single ceremonial moment, permitted to be noticed exactly once per session. Seen-not-noticed is a real state — a well-set line of type, a convincingly rendered metal surface.*

**Lx-3 — Withhold where others display.** Wherever a category convention calls for a display of proof, capability, or momentum, the hero withholds. Departing from a convention is legible as a *choice* only when the convention is well established — logo strips, funding badges, integration icons, and animated counters all qualify. *These devices are legitimate further down, where they function as evidence rather than reassurance.*

**Lx-4 — Material honesty.** Every surface is what it appears to be; no effect simulates a property the surface does not have. §8.5 fixes a narrow vocabulary; §8.3 requires gold in any render to originate from a real reflective or emitting surface rather than a compositing adjustment. Dishonest material is the definitive marker of a mass-market surface. *Not a demand for photorealism — a stylised, obviously-rendered surface is honest; one pretending to a physical property it lacks is not.*

**Lx-5 — The hero spends least.** It holds the lowest allocation of every scarce signal on the page. Signals accumulate value through the scroll: gold at the pricing decision matters *because* it was withheld in the hero. A hero that spends heavily leaves the page's decision moments with nothing to reach for. *Exception: the Ceremonial tier, reserved exclusively for the intro and therefore spent in the hero by definition — once, and never elsewhere.*

## 3. THE SIGNAL BUDGET

| Signal | Hero allocation | Spent instead at | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gold** | Minimal — logo mark and, at most, the single CTA | Result numerals, proof moments, pricing | §6.1, §7.4 |
| **Motion — Ceremonial** | Once, first visit, interruptible | Nowhere — reserved exclusively | Mt-3, §9.5 |
| **Motion — Deliberate** | Not used | Case-study expansion, pricing-tier selection | DSB Ch. 15 |
| **Material intensity** | One hero material, convincingly rendered | Product visualisation, case-study imagery | §8.3, Rd-2 |
| **Typographic scale** | One display-scale element | Section headings step down from it | §6.2 |
| **Proof devices** | None | Portfolio, Case Studies, Testimonials | §13, Lx-3 |
| **Negative space** | **Maximum on the page** | — | Ch. 20 |

The hero is the *most* restrained region on six of seven budgets and the most generous on the seventh. That asymmetry is the luxury strategy in one row: the hero buys its position with space — the only signal whose supply is not scarce and whose perception is not diminished by use.

## 4. POSITIONING COMPARISON

| Decision | Mass-market | Premium | **Luxury** (target) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price visibility | Prominent, compared | Available on a page | Absent until a dedicated section |
| Urgency | Central | Occasional | Permanently forbidden |
| Proof placement | Above the fold | Early | After the claim is provisionally believed |
| CTA count | Many | Two | One |
| Accent colour | Wide, saturated | Consistent, branded | Rationed, ≤10%, functional only |
| Response to a quiet page | Add | Refine | Withhold further |
| Implied relationship | Vendor → shopper | Vendor → buyer | Professional → professional |
| What sells it | Offer | Product quality | Standing |

**The trap.** The luxury column is not "premium, more so." Several cells invert rather than intensify — a premium surface *refines* where a luxury one *withholds*, and refinement applied to something that should have been withheld produces the "expensive-looking" failure.

---

## 5. DO / DON'T

**Do.** Let gold appear exactly once, on the single element where the visitor's action occurs — no gold rule, no gold underline, no gold accent on the eyebrow, no gold in ambient light except where a real reflection justifies it. One appearance makes the colour mean something. Three make it a theme, and a theme cannot mark anything.

**Don't.** Add a "trusted by" logo strip. The most requested hero addition in this category, failing on four counts: it borrows credibility rather than demonstrating it; it consumes a cognitive chunk during comprehension; it presents proof before the visitor has a claim to evaluate it against; and — decisively today — no client names or logos exist in any source document, so it could only be built from placeholder content, which Ch. 27 forbids.

---

## 6. ANTI-PATTERNS

**Luxury by accumulation.** Adding signature devices — a distinctive cursor, a signature transition, a custom scroll, a unique loading treatment — because each contributes character. Detected by counting distinctive devices; more than one means none signals anything.

**Empty scarcity.** Restraint practised where nothing was available to spend. Detected by asking what was consciously declined. Dangerous because it produces a hero that *looks* correct against this chapter while transmitting nothing, and it can be defended in this chapter's own vocabulary.

**The gaudy drift.** Gold expanding gradually — CTA, then underline, then icon, then border, then ambient glow. Detected by measuring gold's share of hero surface at every release, not by reviewing additions. Fixed by reverting to a single functional placement.

---

## 7. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

- [ ] Gold's share of hero surface is measured and well under 10%. *(§6.1)*
- [ ] Every gold appearance is at a functional location, not decorative. *(Lx-1)*
- [ ] §3's signal budget completed with the hero's allocation on each row. *(Lx-5)*
- [ ] No borrowed-credibility device appears. *(Lx-3)*
- [ ] Every material is honest about its own properties; no simulated light source. *(Lx-4)*
- [ ] For each withheld convention, the team can name what was declined and why. *(§6)*
- [ ] No more than one distinctive signature device exists. *(§6)*
- [ ] No luxury signal requires content that does not yet exist. *(§1)*

## 9. STATUS

**The honest limitation.** §1 identifies a real vulnerability: the hero's restraint is meant to signal *"we can afford not to do this,"* but with no published case studies, client names, or metrics, an equally consistent reading is *"they have nothing to show."* Both produce the same hero; the visitor's choice between them is decided by what is further down the page. This is the strongest argument in this Bible for prioritising real proof content, and the limitation closes on its own once such content exists.

**Open question.** §6.1's ≤10% rule applies to "any given screen" — unambiguous for a fixed viewport, less so at ultra-wide, at 320px, and mid-scroll. §7's criterion assumes the hero's initial viewport at each tested width: a reasonable reading, and this Bible's own rather than inherited.
