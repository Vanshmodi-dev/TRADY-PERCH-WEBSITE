# CHAPTER 18 — HERO COPYWRITING PRINCIPLES

**Trady Perch Hero Experience Bible · Part V: Persuasion & Trust**
*First-canonical-draft. Implementation-independent.*

**Inherited From:** Master Vision §16.1–§16.3, §2.2, §5.5, Ch. 19, Ch. 28.
**Governs:** How hero copy is judged — the tests every line must pass, the forbidden registers, the procedure for resolving disputes.
**Does Not Govern:** What the hero actually says. This Bible does not author copy; every example below is illustrative and none is a proposed line.

---

## 1. THE POSITION

Copy is the only channel that says anything explicit. Everything else communicates by implication and can be misread; the claim cannot — it means what it says.

That makes hero copy simultaneously the highest-leverage and most fragile element in this document. A single word can undo the previous seventeen chapters. One exclamation mark reintroduces eagerness. One superlative triggers discounting. One piece of jargon signals concealment. One generic phrase collapses recognition, differentiation, and qualification at once.

**The register, and why it is a capability signal.** §16.1 fixes the voice: write "the way a competent senior engineer explains a solution to a smart but non-technical business owner: clear, direct, respectful of their intelligence, never condescending, never over-explaining." Four constraints in one image — *clear* (wants to be understood, not admired), *direct* (states the thing, then stops), *respectful of intelligence* (no over-explanation), *never condescending* (the owner is smart; they simply do not work on systems all day). And it encodes a capability claim: only someone who genuinely understands a system can describe it simply. **The register is part of the evidence, not a wrapper around the message.**

**The deletion test.** §16.1 supplies the most useful editorial instrument in the source corpus: *"Would a skeptical, busy CEO find this sentence worth their time, or would they skim past it as filler?"* — stated with a consequence: **delete if not.** The consequence is what makes it work. Most copy tests produce a note; this one produces an action.

**Tagline and hero must not duplicate.** §16.3: the hero's line "complements (doesn't repeat) the tagline." The tagline — **"BUILD. AUTOMATE. GROW."** — is a brand statement: three capitalised commands, no object, no audience, no outcome. The tagline says who the company *is*; the hero's claim says what specifically *changes* for this reader. A hero restating the tagline at length has delivered the same content twice and Rung 1 zero times.

## 2. THE THREE HEADLINE TESTS

§16.2's tests, in fixed order. Every candidate passes all three or is rewritten.

**Test 1 — Specificity.** *Could a generic competitor use this exact headline unchanged? If yes, rewrite.* It dominates because it does four jobs at once: produces recognition, differentiates for the comparison shopper, disqualifies non-fit visitors, and is the one credibility signal a template cannot buy. It fails in practice not through obviously empty phrasing — reviewers catch that — but through phrasing that *sounds* specific while remaining portable: "intelligent automation for modern business," "AI systems built for how you actually work." **The mechanical version:** paste the line onto a competitor's site, in your head. If nothing breaks, it fails.

**Test 2 — Benefit.** *Is this an outcome for the reader, or a feature of the company?* Master Vision's illustration: **"Get quotes to customers in minutes, not days"** beats **"AI-powered quoting engine."** The root must be something the visitor already understands: a business owner knows whether quotes take too long; they do not know what a quoting engine is. **The common near-miss** is a company-outcome dressed as a reader-outcome — "We deliver automation that transforms operations" — where the subject is still the company.

**Test 3 — Economy.** *Can any word be cut without losing meaning?* Last, and it must be last: economy applied before specificity produces short, generic lines — the worst outcome, because brevity makes genericness more prominent. Not a word count: a twelve-word line where every word is load-bearing passes; a six-word line with one redundant qualifier fails.

## 3. CORE PRINCIPLES

**Cw-1 — Specificity above all other copy properties.** When any property conflicts with specificity, specificity wins. Every other property — brevity, rhythm, memorability, breadth of appeal — is worth less and frequently in tension with it. *Not a licence for length or jargon: jargon names a category rather than a change, which is the opposite of specific.*

**Cw-2 — The register never modulates.** Headline, qualifier, CTA label, and every other word sit in one voice — including hover states, error copy, and any label reachable from the hero. The practical risk is organisational: these pieces are written by different people at different times and reviewed in isolation.

**Cw-3 — No word the company applies to itself.** No adjective describing the company's own character or standing: *premium, leading, trusted, expert, innovative, world-class, best-in-class, elite, boutique, cutting-edge.* *Adjectives describing the outcome — "faster," "automatic," "manual" — are the substance of a good claim.*

**Cw-4 — Outcomes, never tool-stack.** Name what changes, never which technologies produce it. Tool-stack language also invites a procurement framing — if the buyer can name the components, they can wonder whether they could assemble them. *No exception in the hero; the Technology Stack section is section 3 of 15.*

**Cw-5 — Delete rather than soften.** A line failing the skeptical-CEO test is removed, not rewritten into something milder. Softening filler produces shorter filler; the sentence's problem is that it was not worth reading, which is not a phrasing defect. *Not a bias toward minimal copy — a line that passes stays regardless of length.*

**Cw-6 — Say it once.** No idea appears twice across tagline, headline, qualifier, and CTA. Repetition consumes the load budget without adding a rung, and reads as a lack of confidence in the first statement. *Visual reinforcement of a single stated idea is composition, not repetition.*

---

## 4. THE FORBIDDEN VOCABULARY

| Category | Forbidden | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Hype** | revolutionary, game-changing, 10x, breakthrough, next-generation | Triggers discounting; lowers credibility of adjacent claims |
| **Empty superlatives** | world-class, best-in-class, leading, premier, #1 | Unverifiable; §16.1 forbids by name |
| **Self-description** | trustworthy, premium, expert, innovative, elite, passionate | Assertion in place of demonstration |
| **Buzzword stacking** | "leverage cutting-edge AI-powered synergistic automation" | §16.1's own bad example; reads as concealment |
| **Urgency / scarcity** | act now, limited spots, only 2 left, offer ends, don't miss | Permanently forbidden (§5.5) |
| **Forced warmth** | any exclamation mark, any emoji, "Great question!" | Ch. 19 forbids on the AI surface; the hero holds the same line |
| **Hedging** | we think, it's possible that, we aim to, we strive to | Reads as uncertainty about one's own offering |
| **Tool-stack** | model names, vendor names, platform names, "powered by" | Cw-4, Ac-2 |

## 5. THE COPY DECISION MATRIX

Criteria 1–5 are **gates**: a candidate failing any one is not in the comparison. Only candidates clearing all five are compared on 6–8.

| # | Question | Fatal? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Specific** — would it break on a competitor's site? | **Yes** |
| 2 | **Outcome** — is the subject the reader's business? | **Yes** |
| 3 | **One read** — comprehensible without a second pass? | **Yes** |
| 4 | **Register** — senior engineer explaining plainly? | **Yes** |
| 5 | **Clean** — free of every row in §4? | **Yes** |
| 6 | **Non-duplicating** — distinct from tagline, qualifier, CTA? | Yes — merge or cut |
| 7 | **Economical** — every word load-bearing? | No — trim |
| 8 | **Rhythmic** — reads well aloud? | No — preference |

This prevents the standard failure: a rhythmically superior but generic line winning a review on the strength of how it sounds read aloud in a meeting.

## 6. THE THREE LINES

| Rung | Job | Must | Must not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Claim** | State the transformation | Name a specific operational change in the reader's vocabulary | Describe a service, category, or capability |
| **2 — Qualifier** | Scope the audience | Let a fit visitor recognise themselves and a non-fit one leave | Broaden to retain; list all seven verticals |
| **3 — CTA** | Name the next step | Imply the real cost honestly | Use pressure, hedging, or transactional framing |

**Illustrative pair, to show a test rather than propose a line.** Master Vision's own contrast — *"Get quotes to customers in minutes, not days"* versus *"AI-powered quoting engine"* — passes and fails Test 2 respectively. Note the passing version is *longer*: the clearest demonstration that economy is the third test, not the first.

---

## 7. DO / DON'T

**Do.** Run the deletion test on every sentence at every review, including sentences that have survived before. Copy decays: a line that was specific when the offering was narrower becomes generic as the company's language drifts, and nobody notices because nobody re-reads an approved line. The only mechanism here that catches slow drift rather than bad drafts.

**Don't.** Choose the shorter of two candidates because brevity feels more confident. A short generic line fails Test 1 louder than a long one, because there is nothing else in the sentence to distract from its emptiness. Master Vision's own good example is six words longer than its bad one. When two candidates both pass the five gates, brevity is a legitimate tie-break — and only then.

---

## 8. ANTI-PATTERNS

**The sounds-specific line.** Copy that feels concrete but transfers unchanged to any competitor. Detected only by mechanically applying Test 1. Dangerous because it survives every review conducted by people who already know what the company does — which is everyone in the room.

**Register leakage from the CTA.** Headline and qualifier carefully composed; the CTA label inherited from a component library or written last, in a different voice. Detected by reading all hero copy aloud in sequence as one paragraph.

**Softening under objection.** A specific claim progressively hedged — "we cut quote turnaround from days to minutes" becoming "we help streamline quoting workflows." Detected by comparing each release's claim to the previous. If the specific version is not defensible, make it true or change the claim — do not blur it. A hedged claim fails Tests 1 and 2 simultaneously.

---

## 9. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

- [ ] Every line passes all three headline tests, in order. *(§2)*
- [ ] Test 1 applied mechanically against a competitor context. *(Cw-1)*
- [ ] No word from §4 appears anywhere in the hero. *(§4)*
- [ ] All copy — including the CTA label — sits in one register, verified by reading aloud as a unit. *(Cw-2)*
- [ ] No self-descriptive adjective about the company. *(Cw-3)*
- [ ] No model, vendor, platform, or tool named. *(Cw-4)*
- [ ] No idea appears twice across tagline, claim, qualifier, and CTA. *(Cw-6)*
- [ ] The deletion test run on every sentence at this review, not inherited. *(Cw-5)*
- [ ] Where candidates were compared, the five gates applied before rhythm or brevity. *(§5)*

## 11. STATUS

**Deliberate gap.** This chapter contains no proposed hero copy, and no future revision should. Authoring the claim requires real client language, real engagement history, and finalised positioning. What it supplies is the standard copy is judged against, durable across whatever the copy eventually says.

**Known unresolved inheritance.** Master Vision leaves the primary CTA label explicitly unfinalised — "Book a Call" / "Start a Project," to be "finalized in copy work." Design System Bible Ch. 20 uses "Book a Strategy Call" twice as an illustrative example: strong precedent, not a ratified content decision. Chapter 27 governs how the label is chosen; this chapter governs the register it sits in. Neither resolves it, and neither should.

**Open question.** Whether the qualifier may name specific verticals is unresolved. Naming one or two sharpens recognition dramatically and disqualifies the other five the brand targets; naming all seven exceeds the load budget and reads as a list. The likely resolution is to qualify by *situation* rather than industry — but that is a copy judgment.
