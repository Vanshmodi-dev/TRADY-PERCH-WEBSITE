# CHAPTER 14 — HERO GOALS

**Trady Perch Hero Experience Bible · Part IV: Intent, Goals & Hierarchy**
*First-canonical-draft. Implementation-independent.*

**Inherited From:** Ch. 1 (Ms-1). Master Vision §11.2, §4.3, Ch. 27. UX Blueprint Ch. 6.
**Governs:** What the hero is trying to achieve, in strict priority order; what it is not; how conflicts resolve.
**Does Not Govern:** How each goal is achieved — that is the remaining twenty chapters.

---

## 1. THE POSITION

Most hero failures are not execution failures. They are goal failures: the hero was built to do something it should not have been doing, and did it well.

A goal stack fixes, in advance and in writing, what wins when two reasonable objectives collide. Without one, conflicts are settled by whoever is in the room — and the goal that wins is reliably the one easiest to measure, which is reliably the wrong one.

**Strict priority means strict:** goal 2 is pursued only to the extent it does not damage goal 1. This is a lexicographic ordering, not a weighting — stated that way because weightings get renegotiated under pressure and orderings do not.

```
 G1  BE UNDERSTOOD                              ── non-negotiable
     The visitor can state what the company does.
     ▲ If this fails, nothing below can succeed.
 G2  BE CREDIBLE                                ── non-negotiable
     The visitor believes the company is competent and serious.
     ▲ Achieved non-verbally, before G1 is even read.
 G3  BE RECOGNISED
     The right visitor sees their situation; the wrong one doesn't.
     ▲
 G4  EARN THE SCROLL
     The visitor continues, with interest rather than duty.
     ▲ This is the hero's actual conversion event.
 G5  MAKE THE NEXT STEP VISIBLE
     The visitor knows what action exists, and its cost.
     ▲ Visible ≠ taken. Taking it here is a bonus, not a goal.
```

**G1** — first because every other goal is conditional on it. *Verified by* the five-second paraphrase test. *Sacrificed for* nothing.

**G2** — achieved pre-verbally, before G1's content is read. Ranked second only because a perfectly credible hero communicating nothing has failed more completely than a plain but clear one. *Verified by* the blur test and stress matrix.

**G3** — requires comprehension and is worthless without credibility. A visitor who recognises their problem in a company they distrust leaves with the problem named. **Breadth pressure appears here:** widening the claim damages G3 in the name of reach.

**G4** — the hero's actual conversion event, following automatically from G1–G3. A hero achieving the first three and failing this usually has an exit-level defect — a scroll-hijack, an uninvited widget, a farewell animation — rather than a content one. *Verified by* the exit test and the *quality* of scrolling observed.

**G5** — the most contested ranking here. For a high-consideration purchase, a CTA click at second twelve is a welcome side effect, not the outcome the hero is built for. Ranking G5 last follows from Ms-1. Concretely: if making the CTA more prominent would cost the claim its primary saliency, the CTA loses.

## 2. THE ANTI-GOALS

| Anti-goal | Why not the hero's | Whose job |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximise immediate CTA clicks** | Optimising it damages G1–G4; a click from a visitor who doesn't understand the offering is a wasted sales call | Contact, after twelve sections of evidence |
| **Impress** | Produces "impressive, not inevitable"; attention lands on the design | Nobody's job |
| **Explain everything** | Pre-empts fourteen sections; exceeds the load budget | Solutions, How We Work, Technology |
| **Prove** | Proof before a claim is provisionally believed is discounted | Portfolio, Case Studies, Testimonials |
| **Capture leads** | Any gate, form, or exit-intent device is an eagerness signal and borrows attention it cannot repay | Contact, and the AI Demo's flow |
| **Entertain** | Delight is not this purchase's currency; entertainment fails Hp-6 | Nobody's job |

**On the first row.** It is an anti-goal rather than a low-priority goal deliberately: **pursuing it actively damages the others**, because every device that increases immediate clicks costs saliency, credibility, or composure. A goal that trades against the stack is not a goal.

## 3. CONFLICT RESOLUTION

```
 1. Identify which goals the proposal serves and which it damages.
        ↓
 2. Damages a HIGHER goal than it serves → REJECT.
    No further discussion; the ordering is not a weighting.
        ↓
 3. Serves a higher goal, damages a lower one → ACCEPT,
    and record what was traded so the loss is not re-litigated.
        ↓
 4. Serves and damages goals at the SAME level → under-specified.
    Return it for a version that does not trade within a level.
        ↓
 5. Damages nothing and serves nothing → apply Chapter 2, Test 2.
    Absence of harm is not a reason to include something.
```

**"Make the CTA gold and larger."** Serves G5. Damages G2 (gold above budget reads as gaudy) and G1 (competes for the saliency the claim needs). → **reject at step 2**.

**"Cut the qualifier; the headline alone is punchier."** Serves G1 marginally. Damages G3 decisively — nothing left to recognise oneself in. G1 outranks G3, so step 2 does not trigger; **step 3 catches it**: recording the trade makes visible that a marginal gain in a higher goal is buying an unbounded loss in a lower one.

**"Add a subtle background animation for atmosphere."** Serves nothing. Damages G1 (holds Rung-1 saliency permanently). → **reject at step 2**.

**"Replace the abstract headline with a specific outcome statement."** Serves G1, G3, G4. Damages nothing. → **accept**, no trade to record. Proposals of this shape are what the stack exists to make visible: usually available, usually unglamorous.

## 4. CORE PRINCIPLES

**Hg-1 — The ordering is lexicographic, not weighted.** A lower goal is pursued only where it does not damage a higher one. Weighted priorities are renegotiated at every review because any weighting can be argued upward with sufficient confidence; an ordering can only be changed by amendment. *§3 permits any trade downward; it forbids trading upward without an amendment.*

**Hg-2 — The hero's conversion event is the scroll.** Evaluated on whether the visitor continues, not whether they click. On a high-consideration purchase, the decision the hero can influence is *keep reading*; the decision to book is made after evidence the hero does not carry. *Not indifference to conversion — the contribution is real, indirect, and unmeasurable in isolation.*

**Hg-3 — Every element serves a named goal.** Each element is assigned to G1–G5 in writing; unassignable elements are removed. Ch. 27 requires every homepage section to justify itself against a named list; this applies the discipline one level down. *Exception: ambient elements per Chapter 4's three conditions, which serve G2 collectively.*

**Hg-4 — Anti-goals are not deprioritised goals.** The six are not pursued at any priority, including last. Each trades directly against the stack, so pursuing one "a little" costs proportionally. *Five of the six are important to the business — and are assigned to sections built to serve them.*

## 6. DO / DON'T

**Do.** Record, for every element, which goal it serves — and revisit that record at each review rather than re-deriving it from memory. The record is what makes §3 runnable: without it, every conflict becomes an argument about intent, and arguments about intent are settled by seniority.

**Don't.** Accept a proposal because it will "improve conversion" without identifying which goal it serves and which it damages. Conversion is not on the stack — it is the outcome of the whole page, to which the hero contributes G1 through G4. A proposal that cannot name its goal has skipped the only question the stack exists to ask, and in practice is nearly always serving the first anti-goal.

---

## 7. ANTI-PATTERNS

**Metric capture.** The stack quietly reordering around whatever is measurable — usually CTA click-through, the only hero-adjacent number that exists. Detected by asking which goal the last three changes served; three consecutive G5 changes means the stack has been inverted in practice.

**Goal inflation.** Adding a sixth goal to accommodate a proposal that does not fit — "the hero should also communicate our values," "should also support recruiting." Detected by any goal appearing in review that is not in §1. Fixed by routing the objective to its owning section, or amending properly.

**Silent trading.** Accepting a downward trade at step 3 without recording it, so the loss is invisible and gets re-traded later. Detected by the absence of trade records over a period the hero visibly changed. Repeated unrecorded downward trades produce a hero satisfying G1 alone.

## 8. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

- [ ] Every element is assigned to a named goal, in writing. *(Hg-3)*
- [ ] No element serves an anti-goal. *(Hg-4)*
- [ ] All conflicts this cycle followed §3, with trades recorded. *(Hg-1)*
- [ ] The hero is evaluated on scroll quality, not CTA click-through. *(Hg-2)*
- [ ] G1 verified by the paraphrase test with someone who has not seen the hero. *(G1)*
- [ ] G3 verified by both halves — targets recognise themselves, non-fits self-disqualify. *(G3)*
- [ ] No goal outside §1's five appears in any review record. *(§7)*

## 10. STATUS

The ordering of G1 above G2 is the least certain ranking and is argued rather than settled. A case exists for the reverse: G2 is delivered first in time and colours the reception of G1. The ordering here rests on failure severity — an incomprehensible hero fails completely, a plain-but-clear one partially — and is stated as this Bible's own judgment. In practice the two rarely conflict.

No mechanism exists for measuring G4's *quality* — the difference between a dutiful skim and an interested scroll. Scroll depth and time-on-page conflate the two. Until an instrument exists, G4 is verified by observing real users; §5 says so honestly rather than proposing a proxy metric that would immediately be optimised against.
