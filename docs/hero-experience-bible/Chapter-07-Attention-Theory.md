# CHAPTER 7 — ATTENTION THEORY

**Trady Perch Hero Experience Bible · Part II: The First Fifteen Seconds**
*First-canonical-draft. Implementation-independent.*

**Inherited From:** UX Blueprint Ch. 6 (Attention Ethics). Design System Bible Ch. 40 (Ag-2). Master Vision Ch. 27.
**Governs:** How attention is captured, directed, and spent; the saliency ordering; the ethical limits on capture.
**Does Not Govern:** What the attended element says (Ch. 18) or how it ranks in meaning (Ch. 15) — §4 explains why conflating the two is costly.

---

## 1. THE POSITION

A hero cannot decide what a visitor looks at. It can only decide what is worth looking at, and arrange the field so the most important thing is also the most salient.

Attention in the first second is allocated by a fast, involuntary process that scans the whole field in parallel and flags whatever differs most from its surroundings. The visitor does not choose to look at the moving thing; they have already looked before choosing anything. The uncomfortable consequence: **anything that captures attention does so at the expense of everything else, whether or not it deserved the capture.** There is no additive attention — two salient elements yield one unit, split, plus a tax for the conflict.

**What pre-attentive processing responds to** is a limited set: motion, luminance contrast, colour contrast, orientation, size, enclosure, isolation. Two dominate in a dark, restrained hero:

- **Motion is strongest.** Peripheral motion detection is evolutionarily older and faster than foveal reading — detected outside the fovea, not voluntarily suppressible, and it interrupts reading in progress. **A moving element will be attended to. This is physiology, not a design choice.**
- **Isolation is second, and cheapest.** An element with more empty space around it than anything else is salient without being loud. In a field of uniform darkness and generous space, a single well-set line of type is *the* most salient object present — with no colour, no motion, no decoration. This is the mechanism that makes restrained heroes work at all.

**Attention is borrowed.** Ch. 6: "a visitor's attention is borrowed, not owned," with capture tactics forbidden at the philosophy level. Because motion *will* capture attention, using it for something that does not deserve the capture spends a resource lent for something else — and it is felt: the visitor is redirected, finds nothing, and returns with slightly less patience. Three times and the budget is gone. **Capture is permitted only where the captured attention is repaid.**

**The scan path.** After the first fixation, the eye moves by saliency, reading conventions, and intent. The hero cannot enforce this path — only ensure the path its saliency produces is the same path its meaning requires (Ch. 15). When they agree the hero feels effortless; when they disagree the visitor performs a correction, felt as friction even when it succeeds.

## 2. CORE PRINCIPLES

**At-1 — One element owns primary saliency.** Exactly one element is the most salient thing present, chosen deliberately rather than discovered. Ch. 27's one-dominant-idea rule, expressed perceptually: two competing elements produce a field with no entry point; the eye oscillates, experienced as mild unease with no identifiable cause. *Not "the headline must be biggest" — primary saliency can come from isolation, contrast, or position, and a large low-contrast element in a busy region loses to a small high-contrast one in an empty one.*

**At-2 — Motion outranks everything; budget it accordingly.** Any moving element becomes the primary salient object for as long as it moves, regardless of size, position, or importance. **The corollary teams miss:** a small, subtle, continuously looping ambient animation is not "subtle" in attentional terms — it holds primary saliency permanently, so the headline never gets it. *Exception: motion in direct response to input, which the visitor initiated and is already attending to.*

**At-3 — Saliency is spent, not accumulated.** Adding emphasis to a second element reduces the share held by the first. The intuitive model is additive ("making the CTA gold too will make it more noticeable"); the actual mechanism is competitive. §6.1's ≤10% gold rule is this applied to one channel: gold's saliency is a direct function of its scarcity. *A hero can have a primary and a clearly subordinate secondary; it cannot have two of equal weight.*

**At-4 — Capture must be repaid.** Anything capturing attention involuntarily must deliver value proportionate to the capture. Testable: name what the captured attention received. *If the answer is "atmosphere," it was not repaid.*

**At-5 — The saliency order must match the meaning order.** The sequence elements attract attention in must be the sequence they must be understood in. Where they disagree, the visitor performs an unconscious correction and pays for it in fluency — charged against the company's credibility rather than the layout. *It requires them to be the same element, achievable by making the important thing salient or the salient thing quieter.*

## 3. THE SALIENCY LADDER

```
 RUNG 1 — PRIMARY SALIENCE        exactly one element
   │  Captures the first fixation. Owns the entry point.
   │  Typically: the primary claim.
   ▼
 RUNG 2 — SECONDARY SALIENCE      at most one element
   │  Found second, without searching. Clearly subordinate.
   │  Typically: the CTA, or the qualifier.
   ▼
 RUNG 3 — AVAILABLE ON DEMAND     any number
   │  Found when looked for. Never competes.
   │  Typically: navigation, remaining supporting text.
   ▼
 RUNG 4 — AMBIENT                 uncounted
      Never attended to as an object. Perceived as field.
      Typically: material, light, grain, negative space.
```

**Rules.** Rung 1 holds exactly one element — not zero, not two. Rung 2 holds at most one; nothing there is acceptable and often stronger. Anything that *moves* is on Rung 1 while it moves — so an ambient loop is a Rung-4 element illegitimately occupying Rung 1, the most common saliency defect. Rung 4 elements must satisfy Chapter 4's three ambient conditions; if one is ever *looked at*, it is not ambient.

| Channel | Strength | Cost of use | For this brand |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Motion** | Overwhelming | Very high — captures involuntarily, permanently while active | Reserved for Phase 2; must resolve |
| **Isolation (space)** | High | **None** — space is already required | The primary tool (Ch. 20) |
| **Luminance contrast** | High | Low | Off-white on near-black is already high; little headroom |
| **Colour (gold)** | High | Very high — each use devalues every other | ≤10% surface; minimal in the hero |
| **Size** | Moderate | Moderate — consumes space | Effective only against a calm field |
| **Enclosure** | Moderate | Moderate — a container is another element | Rarely justified |
| **Orientation** | Low here | High — diagonals conflict with composure | Effectively unavailable |
| **Weight (type)** | Low–moderate | Low | Constrained: display type is light-to-regular, never bold |

**The reading for Trady Perch.** The two loudest channels — motion and gold — are the two most tightly rationed by the brand's own rules. The channel with the most headroom is **isolation**, which costs nothing and aligns with every principle in this Bible. Not coincidence: it is why the restraint thesis is coherent rather than merely tasteful.

## 4. SALIENCY IS NOT HIERARCHY

| | **Saliency** (this chapter) | **Hierarchy** (Ch. 15) |
|---|---|---|
| Question | What is noticed first? | What must be understood first? |
| Operates | Pre-attentively, involuntarily | Post-attentively, through structure |
| Determined by | Visual contrast against the field | Meaning, sequence, dependency |
| Failure | Attention lands on the wrong thing | Lands correctly but in the wrong order |
| Fix | Change the field | Change the structure |

**The recurring failure.** A team is told the CTA is the most important element — a hierarchy statement, and a contestable one — so they make it the most salient. The eye now lands on a button before knowing what the company does: Cg-3 violated through a purely visual mechanism. **The resolution:** primary saliency belongs to the claim; the CTA belongs on Rung 2. It does not need to be found first — it needs to be found *when looked for*.

---

## 5. DO / DON'T

**Do.** Achieve primary saliency through isolation — more surrounding emptiness than anything else in the field — rather than size, colour, or motion. It costs nothing from the gold, motion, or cognitive budgets, and it is the only channel that becomes *more* effective as the rest of the hero gets quieter. Every other channel competes; this one compounds.

**Don't.** Add a slow continuous ambient animation because it is "subtle enough not to distract." Attentionally it is not subtle: it holds Rung 1 permanently so the claim never does, and it can never be repaid because it delivers nothing. It also fails Hp-6 — pleasant on the first viewing, a low-grade irritant by the fifth. If the hero needs life, that comes from material and light (Ch. 23, 24), which are perceived without being attended to.

---

## 6. ANTI-PATTERNS

**Saliency inflation.** Responding to "people aren't noticing the CTA" by increasing emphasis, repeatedly. Detected by tracking emphasis changes across releases — an element strengthened three times has a hierarchy problem, not a saliency one. Usually the claim failed, so the visitor never reached the point of wanting an action.

**Ambient capture.** A Rung-4 element acquiring motion, contrast, or interactivity and quietly moving to Rung 1 without anyone deciding. Detected by the blur test — a blurred hero should show the claim's block as the strongest form. Fixed by returning the element to genuine ambience, not by strengthening the claim to compete.

**Attention borrowed against the future.** Capturing attention with a device promising something the page will not deliver — an implied video, demo, or statistic. Detected by At-4's repayment test. The debt is paid downstream, where the disappointment is attributed to the whole company.

## 7. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

- [ ] The saliency ladder is declared, with a named element on each occupied rung. *(§3)*
- [ ] Exactly one element on Rung 1; at most one on Rung 2. *(At-1)*
- [ ] Nothing loops, pulses, or moves continuously after Phase 2 resolves. *(At-2)*
- [ ] Every attention-capturing element has a named repayment. *(At-4)*
- [ ] Saliency order and meaning order are identical, both written down. *(At-5)*
- [ ] The blur test shows the intended Rung-1 element as the strongest form. *(Ch. 6, Test 1)*
- [ ] Gold is within budget and not competing for Rung 1. *(At-3)*
- [ ] Every Rung-4 element satisfies Chapter 4's three ambient conditions. *(§3)*

## 9. STATUS

§3's relative-strength column is ordered by general perceptual principles and this brand's constraints, not by measurement against this brand's hero. Ordering between adjacent rows — particularly isolation versus luminance contrast — could differ under a specific composition; the two ends are robust.

This chapter does not address attention over longer horizons — return-visit attention, banner blindness across repeat exposures, or the cost of a hero seen among twenty tabs. All are relevant to a considered purchase with multiple visits, and no source document currently covers them.
