# CHAPTER 8 — VISUAL STORYTELLING

**Trady Perch Hero Experience Bible · Part II: The First Fifteen Seconds**
*First-canonical-draft. Implementation-independent.*

**Inherited From:** Master Vision Ch. 9 (diegetic motion), §8.1–§8.5, Ch. 15, §13. Design System Bible Ch. 1 (P6), Ch. 40 (Ag-4).
**Governs:** The hero's role in the site's narrative; what it may narrate; the standard every visual element must meet.
**Does Not Govern:** The story the case studies tell, the copy carrying it (Ch. 18), or the motion expressing it (Ch. 21).

---

## 1. THE POSITION

The homepage is a fifteen-section argument in a fixed order, each section answering one unspoken question and installing one feeling. That gives the hero a limited role.

**The hero is the first sentence, not the synopsis.** This rules out the two most common conceptions at once. Not a summary — a summary answers questions not yet asked and spends every reveal the page below needed. Not a self-contained pitch — one that works alone implies the fourteen sections after it are redundant. A first sentence has two jobs: **establish the world, and make the second sentence inevitable.**

**Showing, because telling is largely prohibited.** Ch. 28 forbids the brand asserting its own qualities; Ch. 27 item 7 forbids claims without nearby evidence. What remains is showing — and the hero has no room for evidence. It cannot show a case study, a metric, or a testimonial; pulling those in spends the page's proof at the moment it carries least weight.

So the hero shows through **the only material it has: itself.** Its composure demonstrates composure; its precision demonstrates precision; its restraint demonstrates that the company does not need to persuade. Its story is told in the first person, present tense, by behaviour.

**The diegetic standard, extended to stillness.** Ch. 9 requires every animation to be diegetic; Ag-4 enforces it procedurally — a one-sentence answer to "what does this represent?" before any timing discussion, with "it looks good" explicitly insufficient. This chapter extends the standard to every visual element, including static ones. The argument for diegetic motion is that motion is read as intent whether or not intent was designed; the same is true of every visual choice. A material implies a claim about substance; a light source implies a point of view; a crop implies what is being looked at. An undesigned one still communicates — it communicates something nobody chose.

**The hero's diegetic test:** every element must answer *"what is this telling the visitor about the company, and is that true?"* Elements answering "nothing" fail Test 2; elements answering something unsubstantiable fail Ms-2.

## 2. WHAT THE HERO IS FORBIDDEN TO NARRATE

Each is a real story this brand tells; none belongs in the hero.

| Story | Belongs in | Why not the hero |
|---|---|---|
| The problem, vividly | Problems We Solve (§13.4) | Agitation before composure raises resistance (Ej-2) |
| What we build, enumerated | Solutions (§13.5) | A list has no dominant idea (Hp-4) |
| Who we've done it for | Industries, Portfolio | Proof lands only after a claim is provisionally believed |
| How we work | How We Work (§13.7) | Answers a question not yet asked |
| Proof and numbers | Case Studies (§13.9) | The highest-trust content type, wasted at the lowest-trust moment |
| What it costs | Pricing Philosophy | Explicitly forbidden anywhere earlier (§5.4) |
| Who we are | Deferred entirely (§14.2) | A thin origin story damages credibility |
| Technology used | Technology Stack (§13.3) | Contradicts the outcomes-language rule (§13.5) |

The hero narrates one thing: **what changes for the visitor's business, and who that is true for.**

---

## 3. CORE PRINCIPLES

**Vs-1 — The hero is a first sentence.** It establishes the world and creates the necessity of the next section — nothing more. §13's fixed order is a sequence in which each section depends on the one before; a hero pre-empting sections 3–6 removes their reason to exist. *A good first sentence is complete; it simply is not the whole story.*

**Vs-2 — Every element is diegetic.** Every visual element, moving or static, answers "what does this represent?" before it is considered. The *ordering* matters: once an element is beautifully executed, sunk cost makes removal political rather than a design question. *"This represents the calm of a system running correctly" is valid; "this is a gradient because gradients look modern" is not.*

**Vs-3 — Show through behaviour, not depiction.** Depiction is a claim in visual form, and claims are discounted. A hero depicting *intelligence* through a neural graphic asserts it; one that responds instantly, degrades gracefully, and says precisely what it means demonstrates it. §6.4's ban on robot imagery — it "makes the brand look like it is selling science fiction rather than operational software" — is this applied to one recurring case. *Not a ban on imagery: §8.2 permits abstract, dark, material photography and renders, which are surface and atmosphere, not depictions of claimed qualities.*

**Vs-4 — No metaphor the visitor must decode.** Cg-4: ambiguity is the most expensive element type, recruiting the slow system with no guaranteed resolution. A metaphor is a puzzle presented to a visitor who did not agree to solve one, in the window where patience is lowest. Even a *successfully* decoded metaphor cost more than it returned. *The alternative is not literal illustration — it is material and light with no representational content, which is what §8.2 already specifies.*

**Vs-5 — The hero must make the next section necessary.** It succeeds when scrolling feels like the continuation of a sentence, not the start of a task. A scroll happens when a question has been raised and not answered. A hero answering everything produces a scroll of duty; one raising exactly one well-formed question produces a scroll of interest. *Not licence for a cliffhanger — withholding to manufacture curiosity is manipulation and is detected. The question must be raised honestly, by being specific about the outcome and silent about the method.*

## 4. THE HERO'S NARRATIVE POSITION

```
 1  NAVIGATION      — "you are somewhere organised"
 2  ► HERO ◄        — "here is what changes, and for whom"
    ────────────────  world established · question raised
                      the hero's jurisdiction ends here
 3  TECH STACK      — "and we're technically real"
 4  PROBLEMS        — "we know your specific friction"
 5  SOLUTIONS       — "here is what we build"
 6  INDUSTRIES      — "for businesses like yours"
 7  HOW WE WORK     — "through a repeatable method"
 8  PORTFOLIO       — "we have actually done this"
 9  CASE STUDIES    — "with measurable outcomes"
10  AI DEMO         — "and you can try it yourself"
11  TESTIMONIALS    — "other people agree"
12  PRICING         — "here is how money works"
13  FAQ             — "and here are your remaining doubts"
14  CONTACT         — "here is how to start"
15  FOOTER          — "we are a real, findable company"
```

Every section below answers a question the visitor has not asked yet. The hero's discipline keeps those questions unasked and therefore answerable. **The test:** for any proposed element, find the section that already owns that content. If one exists, the element is being *relocated*, not added.

## 5. THE STORY SPINE

| Beat | Content | Carried by | Verified by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The world** | This company builds operational systems, and is serious | Material, light, space, composure, timing — *not language* | Blur test |
| **2. The change** | Something specific becomes different for the business | The primary claim | Paraphrase test |
| **3. The address** | This is for a business like yours | The qualifier's specificity | State 4 |

**Beat 1 is carried entirely non-verbally and lands first.** It is the beat most often left undesigned, because it has no copy to review and no component to build — and it determines how beats 2 and 3 are received.

## 6. VISUAL VOCABULARY

| Permitted | Forbidden |
|---|---|
| Abstract, dark, moody photography or renders | Stock photography of business people shaking hands |
| Subtle metallic textures; soft light studies | Teams high-fiving at a laptop |
| Dark architectural or workspace photography | Generic diverse office team photography |
| Thin-stroke, geometric, monoline iconography | Filled or glyph icons; mascots; emoji |
| One dominant, soft-but-directional light source | Lightbulbs as ideas |
| Deep retained shadow | **Robot / humanoid AI imagery** — a category cliché |
| Material realism over geometric abundance | Swirling abstract 3D; a dozen floating shapes |
| Longer-lens optical character | Wide-angle distortion; dramatic low hero angles |
| Light film grain throughout | Neon; saturated blue/purple "tech" accents |
| Gold as a genuine light source or reflection | Gold as a colour-grade filter or decorative fill |
| Screenshots inside a minimal dark device frame | Raw, unframed screenshot rectangles |

## 7. DO / DON'T

**Do.** Let the hero's visual content be *nothing but surface* — material, light, grain, space, no representational content — and carry the whole story in three lines of type. This satisfies Vs-3, Vs-4, Chapter 7's ladder, and §8.2's art direction at once. It is also the hardest option to execute convincingly, which is exactly why it persuades: an unconvincing material has nowhere to hide.

**Don't.** Build an abstract animated visualisation of "data flowing through a system" as the centrepiece. It fails Vs-4 (decoding), Vs-3 (depicts rather than demonstrates), At-2 (permanent motion holds Rung 1), and Rd-1's "fewer objects, more convincingly." It also pre-empts section 10 — the Interactive AI Demo, "the single highest-leverage proof-of-capability moment on the homepage." A depiction of capability in the hero devalues an actual demonstration of it eight sections later.

---

## 8. ANTI-PATTERNS

**Synopsis creep.** The hero acquiring a compressed version of the whole page — a value proposition, then bullets, then a logo strip, then a stat — each defended as "the most important thing from section N." Detected with §4's test. The pressure to summarise usually indicates a weak beat 2.

**Decoration with a story attached retroactively.** An element built because it looked good, then given a diegetic justification at review. Detected procedurally, not visually: Ag-4 requires the justification *before* the work, so one arriving after is a rationalisation.

**Metaphor as a substitute for specificity.** Reaching for an abstract visual of "automation" precisely because the claim is not specific enough to stand alone. Detected by removing the visual and asking whether the hero still says anything. Fixed in the copy, not the composition.

---

## 9. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

- [ ] Every element has a written diegetic answer recorded *before* it was built. *(Vs-2)*
- [ ] No element duplicates content owned by a section below. *(Vs-1, §4)*
- [ ] No visual metaphor requiring interpretation. *(Vs-4)*
- [ ] No forbidden item from §6 appears. *(§6)*
- [ ] The three story beats are named, with the means carrying them. *(§5)*
- [ ] Beat 1 is designed explicitly, not left as a by-product. *(§5)*
- [ ] Exactly one honest, unanswered question is raised. *(Vs-5)*
- [ ] No quality of the company is depicted rather than demonstrated. *(Vs-3)*

## 11. STATUS

§4's diagram assumes §13's fifteen-section order remains fixed. If the homepage is restructured, this chapter's central mechanism needs re-deriving rather than carrying over.

**Open question.** Whether the hero may contain a *live* element that demonstrates rather than depicts — a genuinely working micro-interaction, as distinct from an animation representing something real — is unresolved. It would satisfy Vs-3 more completely than anything currently permitted, and it conflicts with Cg-3 and At-2. Not adjudicated here; resolve only against a concrete proposal, since it interacts directly with the AI Demo's placement in section 10.
