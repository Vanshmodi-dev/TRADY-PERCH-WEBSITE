## 1. THE POSITION

**In a category saturated with claims, the most credible available signal is the visible decision not to make one.**

A bet with a real downside, stated in §1 as "restraint is the strongest form of persuasion available to a technical company." A restrained hero forfeits the arousal of a bold one, loses a first-glance beauty contest against an animated gradient, and converts fewer low-intent visitors. Those costs are accepted knowingly — and a builder who does not understand why will erode them one small addition at a time.

**The rejected default.** The standard AI-company hero: a bold transformation claim, a blue-to-purple gradient, a particle field, two or three CTAs, a "trusted by" strip, a funding badge. Rejected not because it is common but because of what it concedes — that the company expects not to be believed and is compensating with volume. Every element is a volume control: the claim compensates for absent proof, the gradient for an absent material identity, the particles for an absent product, the multiple CTAs for an absent recommendation, the logo strip for an absent case study.

Ch. 28 supplies the test: *"does this make Trady Perch look like it needs to try harder to be believed, or does it make Trady Perch look like it already doesn't need to?"*

**Why quiet reads as strong.** Persuasion effort is legible, and effort correlates inversely with perceived position — the party that must convince you is the weaker one. This is why a private bank's materials are quieter than a payday lender's, and it is exactly the stated positioning. Restraint is persuasion by demonstrated indifference to any single visit's outcome — credible only if the underlying calm is real. Consistency makes the signal expensive to fake, and expensive-to-fake signals are the only ones carrying information.

| Restraint wins | Restraint costs |
|---|---|
| Credibility with a buyer screening for hype | Immediate arousal and shareability |
| Differentiation where everyone else is loud | First-glance impact in a bake-off |
| Durability — quiet ages far slower (Ch. 32) | Raw conversion from low-intent traffic |
| Alignment between surface and product | Internal comfort — hard to defend in a meeting |
| A hero that survives being seen twice | Competing on volume during a campaign |

## 2. CORE PRINCIPLES

**Hp-1 — Inevitable, not impressive.** The target impression is that it could not reasonably have been otherwise. Ch. 28: the site should feel "inevitable. Not impressive." Impressiveness invites evaluation of the design — attention on the wrong object. *Test: ask a reviewer to describe the hero from memory an hour later. Correct outcome — they remember what the company does and cannot recall the design.*

**Hp-2 — Subtraction is the default operation.** When a hero is not working, the first hypothesis is that something must be removed (§3.1: "if in doubt, remove it"). It must be a *default* because additions have an advocate and a stated purpose, while the clarity they damage has none. *Exception: a hero failing Q1 is under-specified, and the fix is genuinely additive.*

**Hp-3 — The hero is a sample, not an advertisement.** An advertisement is judged on what it claims; a sample on what it *is*. For a company selling built systems a sample is far more persuasive, and the only one of the two verifiable in fifteen seconds. *The sample is the hero's own behaviour — timing, correctness, response to input, handling of a slow connection — not a product screenshot.*

**Hp-4 — One screen, one idea, one motion.** Ch. 27's non-negotiable, reinforced by Ag-2's three-element animation ceiling. Two dominant ideas is a confession that the company has not decided which matters.

**Hp-5 — Silence is a component.** Empty space is an active element with a stated job, never leftover area. The most common way a good hero degrades is that its space is treated as available inventory; once unallocated, every future request has somewhere to go. Developed in Chapter 20.

**Hp-6 — The hero must survive a second viewing.** Every element must remain acceptable on the fifth viewing, not merely tolerable on the first. §5.1's buyer returns, often with a colleague watching. This is the reasoning behind the intro's once-per-session rule (§9.5), generalised. *Not a ban on delight — a requirement that delight be durable. Well-set type still satisfies at the fiftieth viewing; a bouncing entrance irritates by the third.*

## 3. THE FOUR STANDING TESTS

Argued in order. A "no" is fatal, not negotiable.

```
 TEST 1 — THE TRYING TEST                         (MV Ch. 28)
 Does this make the company look like it needs to
 try harder to be believed?                   → if yes, cut
                       ↓
 TEST 2 — THE SUBTRACTION TEST               (Hp-2, MV §3.1)
 If removed, what specifically breaks?
 "It would feel empty" is not an answer.     → if none, cut
                       ↓
 TEST 3 — THE SKEPTIC TEST                        (MV §16.1)
 Would a skeptical, busy CEO find this worth
 their time, or skim past it as filler?    → if filler, cut
                       ↓
 TEST 4 — THE FIFTH-VIEWING TEST                      (Hp-6)
 Still acceptable on the fifth visit?     → if not, redesign
                       ↓
               Eligible for the hero
```

Test 1 is first because it is cheapest and most often decisive. Test 2 is second because it catches elements that pass Test 1 by being inoffensive rather than necessary — most hero bloat enters this way.

## 4. THREE HERO POSTURES

| | **The Claimant** (rejected) | **The Showman** (rejected) | **The Operator** (adopted) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening move | Asserts a transformation | Performs spectacle | States what it does, plainly |
| Implied relationship | Seller → prospect | Performer → audience | Professional → professional |
| Proof strategy | Superlatives, badges, logos | Complexity as evidence of skill | Restraint plus a payable promise |
| Emotional target | Excitement | Awe | Recognition, then relief |
| Under scrutiny | Nothing behind the claim | Effort disproportionate to substance | The same thing it showed at a glance |
| Failure mode | Reads as a freelancer portfolio | Reads as a demo reel | Reads as under-ambitious internally |
| In 3 years | Badly — claims date fastest | Very badly — effects date fastest | Slowly |

The Operator is not a compromise between the other two. It plays a different game: they compete for attention, it competes for credibility, and the two are frequently opposed.

---

## 5. DO / DON'T

**Do.** Remove a well-executed element because nothing specific breaks in its absence — and record that it went under Test 2 rather than for poor quality, so the reasoning survives for the next person who proposes it.

**Don't.** Add a supporting element to a hero that "feels empty," where that is the whole justification. It fails Test 2 by definition and is the main mechanism by which a governed hero becomes ungoverned. An empty-feeling hero almost always has an under-resolved central idea; resolve that idea rather than surrounding it with company.

---

## 6. ANTI-PATTERNS

**Restraint as an alibi.** An under-developed hero presented as a minimal one. Detected by running Test 2 in reverse: if removing an element breaks nothing *and* the hero still cannot answer Q1, no idea was ever formed. Dangerous because this Bible's own vocabulary defends it.

**Trend adoption by proxy.** Justifying an element by pointing at a respected peer. Master Vision names Apple, Stripe, Linear, Vercel, Framer, Tesla, Nothing, and Arc as *posture* references — the transferable part is that each "made the deliberate choice to look less busy than their competitors while charging more." Copying a device without the reasoning imports the artefact and leaves the discipline.

**Governance by taste.** Resolving disputes by seniority or confidence. Detected when review notes contain preferences without a named test. Fixed by requiring every objection to cite a standing test or numbered principle.

---

## 7. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

- [ ] Every element has survived all four standing tests, recorded rather than assumed. *(§3)*
- [ ] The dominant idea and dominant motion are each named in writing. *(Hp-4)*
- [ ] No element's justification is "it would feel empty otherwise." *(Hp-2, Hp-5)*
- [ ] Empty regions have a stated job. *(Hp-5)*
- [ ] Every animated element evaluated at its fifth viewing. *(Hp-6)*
- [ ] Every review objection on record cites a principle or standing test. *(§6)*

## 9. STATUS

The cost column in §1 is asserted from category reasoning, not measured — no A/B evidence exists comparing a restrained hero against a conventional one for this brand. Should such evidence emerge, a conversion lift for a louder hero would be evidence about *one metric*, not a refutation of the positioning bet (Ms-1). §4 also assumes the visitor can distinguish the Operator posture from simple under-investment; whether real traffic skews sophisticated enough is unknown.
